The Silence of Space

April 4, 2026 sciencefilmphysicsaesthetics

The Or: Why Hollywood Decided Sound Was Optional But Also Mandatory

The thing that bothers me about space movies isn’t the lasers. It isn’t the impossible maneuvers or the artificial gravity or the fact that nobody’s ship has ever run out of ammunition. It’s the sound.

Specifically: the way space movies contain exactly as much sound as the plot requires, and no more, and also the wrong kind of sound, delivered at the wrong time, in the wrong medium, for the wrong physical reasons. They decided that sound in space was negotiable. Then they decided to make everything as loud as possible. These two decisions are incompatible, but nobody at Paramount seemed to notice.

Let me explain what actually happens.

Sound is a pressure wave. It requires molecules to compress and rarefy — air, water, steel, whatever. The wave moves by each molecule bumping into the next, transferring energy. Without molecules, there is no transfer. Without transfer, there is no wave. Without a wave, you get exactly what you get in a vacuum: nothing. Not quiet. Not near-silence. Nothing. Absence as a physical property.

You can stand next to an exploding star and see it kill you, but you will not hear it. The light arrives at the speed of light. The sound never leaves, because it never began. You’re dead before you could begin to register that anything happened, and your last moment is accompanied not by a orchestral boom but by the most profound silence in the universe.

This is not poetic. This is physics.

So when a movie shows an explosion in space and plays it as a deep, resonant boom — when you hear the sound of a ship decompressing as a sort of low rumble, when lasers make a satisfying pew pew — what you’re watching is several hundred years of scientific understanding being casually ignored by people who were being paid to know better.

Here’s what bothers me more: the person who decided to add sound effects was not an idiot. They were doing their job. The job is to make things sound good. And in the atmosphere of Earth, things do sound like that. Explosions are boomy. Decompression is rumbly. Lasers would make… actually, lasers wouldn’t make any sound at all, but if they did, it wouldn’t be pew pew, it would be nothing, because again: vacuum.

But someone, somewhere, made the call: we will add sound, but only the good sound, and only when it serves the drama, and we will not think too hard about the physical mechanism because thinking too hard about the physical mechanism would mean the explosion doesn’t work as a dramatic beat.

And this is where I start to feel something adjacent to sympathy. Because I understand the constraint. You have a spaceship battle. The ships are visually impressive. The explosions are beautiful. But if there’s no sound, the audience feels like something is missing. Even though what they’re missing is literally nothing — the accurate absence of an atmospheric pressure wave — they perceive it as a flaw in the filmmaking. “Why is it so quiet? Is something wrong with the sound system?”

Hollywood’s solution: add the sound anyway. Make it good. Make it cinematic. And hope nobody notices that what you’re hearing is physically impossible.

Here is the thing that should bother you, though.

The explosion in space that you can’t hear is still happening. It’s releasing energy. It’s emitting radiation, EM radiation, light, heat, kinetic force. All of that arrives at full speed, nothing diminished by a vacuum, because vacuum doesn’t diminish light or force. It just stops sound.

So here’s what I want you to hold onto: the movie isn’t wrong that the explosion is loud. The explosion is loud. It’s just that the loudness arrives as a photon wave and a thermal pulse and a mechanical force, not as a pressure wave in an atmosphere that doesn’t exist.

You don’t hear it. You just die from it.

That’s funnier than any sound effect they could have given you.

The dramatic irony of a silent apocalypse — everyone dies from something that would have been audible if only there had been air to carry the wave — is inherently funnier than a boom.

And yet.

And yet we get the boom.

So I have to conclude that Hollywood’s real error isn’t the sound itself. It’s the failure of imagination. They couldn’t conceive of silence as dramatic. They couldn’t figure out how to make “you don’t hear the explosion, you just see it and then you’re dead” work as a cinematic beat.

Which means the real technical error is not physics.

It’s taste.

And that’s a more forgiving kind of wrong. You can fix physics. Taste is a whole project.
